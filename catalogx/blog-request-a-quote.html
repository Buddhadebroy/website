Most WooCommerce stores are built around one idea: a shopper sees a price, adds the product to the cart and pays. That works well for a T-shirt or a phone case. It works badly for a pallet of tiles, a custom printed banner, a hotel's worth of furniture or a machine part that needs a spec check before anyone can say what it costs.

For those products, the fixed price on the page is either wrong, scary or missing. The buyer wants to talk first. They want to know about volume discounts, delivery dates, materials or whether the product fits their use. If the only button on the page says Add to Cart, many of them leave and email three of your competitors instead.

A Request a Quote button fixes that. It turns a product page into the start of a conversation that ends in an order. In this guide we will cover what the button really does, which stores should use it, the different ways to add it to WooCommerce, a full step by step setup with CatalogX, and the habits that turn more quote requests into paid orders.

You do not need to be a developer to follow along. Every step here is done from the WordPress admin, and you can start with the free version of the plugin.

What a Request a Quote button is (and what it is not)

A Request a Quote button is a button on a product page, shop page or cart that sends the store a price request instead of starting a payment. The shopper picks the product, the variation and the quantity, adds a few details such as their name, company and a note, and sends it. The store reviews the request and replies with a price.

In a good setup, that is not the end. The quote becomes a document the buyer can see in their account, download as a PDF, accept or reject. When they accept, the quote turns into a normal WooCommerce order, so stock, invoices, shipping and reports all keep working the way they already do.

It helps to be clear about what a quote button is not:

  • It is not a contact form. A contact form sends a message with no product, no quantity and no variation. You then have to email back and ask for all the details. A quote request carries the product data with it, so you can price it straight away.
  • It is not just hiding the price. Hiding prices alone leaves the buyer with nothing to do. A quote button gives them a clear next step.
  • It does not have to replace your checkout. Many stores show Add to Cart and Request a Quote side by side. Small buyers pay now, big buyers ask for a better deal.

Enquiry or quote? An enquiry is a question ("Does this come in oak?"). A quote is a price request ("What is your price for 40 of these?"). CatalogX has both. Use enquiries when buyers mostly have questions, and quotes when they mostly want a price. Many stores turn on both.

When a quote beats a checkout

A checkout is the fastest path to money when the price is fixed and the buyer is sure. A quote is the better path when either of those is not true. Here are the store types where a quote button usually pays for itself.

1. Wholesale and B2B stores

Business buyers rarely pay the list price. They expect a price based on volume, payment terms and how often they order. Showing them the retail price can push them away, because it looks like you do not deal with businesses. A quote button tells them you do, and lets you price each account properly.

2. Custom and made-to-order products

Printed merchandise, signs, engraved gifts, custom furniture and tailored clothing all have prices that depend on choices the buyer makes. You can try to build a giant product configurator that covers every option, or you can let the buyer describe what they need and send a quote. For most small teams, the quote is faster to launch and easier to keep accurate.

3. High-value items

Nobody adds a 9,000 dollar espresso machine to a cart on a whim. Buyers of expensive products want reassurance, finance options or a bundle deal. A quote gives your sales team a reason to talk to them and a chance to answer the doubts that stop them from buying.

4. Products with changing costs

If your cost depends on raw materials, freight or exchange rates, a fixed price on the page is either too high (you lose the sale) or too low (you lose money). Quotes let you price on the day, with an expiry date so the price does not stay open forever.

5. Bulk and event orders

A café ordering 300 cups, a school ordering uniforms or a company ordering conference gifts will all ask about discounts. Rather than making them email you, let them build a quote list with all the products and quantities, then send one request.

6. Services sold through WooCommerce

Installation, repairs, cleaning and consulting are often sold as products in WooCommerce. The real price depends on the job. A quote button with a short form (size of space, address, preferred date) gets you the details you need to price it.

7. Stores that sell to both retail and trade

This is the most common case we see. A store sells to the public at a fixed price and to trade buyers at a negotiated one. You can show Add to Cart to everyone and add a quote button for logged-in trade buyers, or show the quote button only on certain categories.

8. Catalog-only stores

Some businesses do not want online payment at all. Manufacturers, distributors and showroom brands use WooCommerce as a product catalog. For them, a quote button is the main way the website makes money, because every request goes straight to the sales team.

Signs your store needs quotes

Not sure if this applies to you? Look for these signs in your inbox and your analytics.

  • You get emails that start with "What is your best price for..." or "Can you do a discount on...".
  • Shoppers add many units to the cart, then abandon at checkout.
  • Your sales team builds quotes in a spreadsheet or Word file and emails them by hand.
  • You often change prices on an order after it is placed, because the buyer had a deal with you.
  • Some products have "Contact us for price" written in the description.
  • Buyers ask for a formal quote document for their purchasing department before they can pay.

If two or more of those are true, a quote button will save you time and most likely win you orders you are losing today.

Quote, enquiry or catalog mode: which do you need?

CatalogX has three tools that often get mixed up. They solve different problems, and many stores use two or all three together.

Catalog mode

Hides Add to Cart, and with Pro, prices. Use it when online checkout does not fit some or all of your products.

Enquiry

Lets shoppers ask a question about a product. Use it when buyers need answers before they are ready to buy.

Quotation

A simple way to decide: if your inbox is full of questions, start with enquiries. If it is full of price requests, start with quotes. If buyers keep checking out at prices that are wrong for them, add catalog mode to the products where that happens.

One common pattern for growing stores is to start with catalog mode and quotes on a single category, such as bulk packs or custom orders, while the rest of the store sells as usual. Once the team is comfortable replying to quotes, they widen the rules to more products.

Three ways to add a quote button to WooCommerce

WooCommerce does not include a quote feature out of the box. You have three broad choices.

Option 1: Custom code

A developer can remove the Add to Cart button with a WooCommerce hook and print a link to a form instead. This is cheap at the start, but the work grows fast. You will need to store the requests somewhere, email them, handle variations and quantities, let the buyer see their quote, and turn an accepted quote into an order. Every WooCommerce update is a chance for something to break.

Option 2: A general form plugin

You can put a form plugin on each product page with a "Get a quote" form. It is quick to set up, but the form does not know about the product. The buyer has to type the product name, the variation and the quantity by hand, and your team has to copy that into an order later. There is no quote list, no PDF quote and no way to accept the quote online.

Option 3: A dedicated quote plugin

A plugin built for WooCommerce quotes, such as CatalogX, adds the button, keeps the product data with the request, gives the buyer a quote list, and lets you turn quotes into orders. This is the route most stores should take, and it is the one we will use for the rest of this guide.

    When a request comes in, open it, set your price per line, add a discount, tax and shipping, and send it. You can also create a quote from scratch for a buyer who called or emailed you, so every deal lives in one place.

Designing a quote form buyers will finish

The quote form is where most requests are won or lost. Every extra field is a reason to give up. Every missing field is an extra email you have to send later. Here is how to find the balance.

Start with what you cannot price without

List the details you truly need to give a price. For most stores that is just the product, variation, quantity, name and email. The product, variation and quantity are already filled in by the plugin. So the buyer often only types two things. That is a form people finish.

Add fields only for real pricing questions

If you ship heavy goods, you need a delivery postcode. If you print logos, you need artwork. If you sell to businesses, a company name helps you check their account. With the Pro form builder, you can add text, dropdown, checkbox, radio, date and file fields, and mark each one as required or optional.

A good test: for each field, ask "Would I change the price because of this answer?" If the answer is no, make the field optional or remove it.

Use dropdowns instead of free text

"When do you need it?" as a free text field gets answers like "soon", "ASAP" and "before the event". A dropdown with "This week, This month, Next 3 months, Just researching" gets you answers you can sort. It also tells you which leads to call first.

Let buyers attach files

For custom work, a file upload is the single most useful field. A drawing, a logo or a spec sheet answers ten questions at once. CatalogX Pro supports file attachments on enquiries, so buyers can send them with the request.

Set expectations right on the form

Add one line under the submit button that says when they will hear back. "We reply within one business day" turns a nervous click into a confident one. Then make sure you keep that promise.

Protect it from spam

Any public form attracts bots. Turn on CAPTCHA and spam protection so your sales team does not waste time on fake requests. It takes a minute and saves hours.

Replying to quotes: the habits that win orders

Getting quote requests is only half the job. The other half is replying in a way that makes the buyer say yes. These habits matter more than any setting.

Reply fast

A buyer who asks you for a quote has probably asked others too. The first clear, complete reply has a big advantage. Set a target, such as same day for requests before 3 PM and next morning for the rest. Turn on admin notifications so new requests do not sit unseen in a queue.

Keep every conversation in one place

Quotes that live across three inboxes and a spreadsheet get lost. With the Enquiry Portal in CatalogX Pro, every request and reply sits in one inbox, with tags, pins and attachments. Anyone on your team can pick up a conversation without asking "what did we say to this customer?".

Make the quote easy to read

A good quote shows each product, the quantity, the unit price, any discount, tax, shipping and the total. It shows the expiry date and your payment terms. It does not hide fees in small print. If the buyer has to email you to understand the quote, you have already slowed the deal down.

Explain the price when it helps

If you gave a volume discount, say so. "Price includes 12 percent off for 200 units or more" does two things. It shows the buyer they got a deal, and it nudges them toward the next tier if they are close.

Offer two or three options

When it makes sense, quote a good, better and best option. For example, standard delivery and express delivery, or a basic finish and a premium finish. Buyers like to choose, and a choice makes it less likely they go elsewhere to compare.

Follow up once, kindly

Many quotes go quiet not because the buyer said no, but because they got busy. A short follow-up two or three days later wins back a good share of them. Something like: "Just checking you got the quote for the 40 oak tables. Happy to adjust quantities or delivery dates if that helps." If the quote has an expiry date, mention it.

Learn from rejections

When a buyer rejects a quote, ask why in one line. Was it price, timing or something else? Over a few months, the answers will tell you more about your pricing than any report.

How to get more quote requests

Once the button is live, the next question is how to get more buyers to use it. Most of the gains come from making the button easy to find and safe to click.

Put the button where buying decisions happen

The product page is the obvious place, but not the only one. Add the quote button to category pages for products that are often bought in bulk, and to landing pages built for trade buyers. With blocks, you can also add a quote button inside a blog post or a buying guide, right where a reader decides they are interested.

Say who the quote is for

A short line next to the button makes a big difference. "Buying 20 or more? Request a quote for volume pricing." tells the buyer that asking is normal and that it will probably save them money. Without that line, some buyers assume the quote button is only for very large companies.

Show trust near the button

Buyers who are about to share their details want to know they will be treated well. A reply time promise, a note that there is no obligation, and a short line like "Trusted by 300 trade customers" near the button all reduce the worry of clicking. Use only claims that are true for your store.

Tell existing customers

Your past buyers are the easiest source of quote requests. Mention the new option in your next newsletter, in order confirmation emails and in your team's email signatures. Many repeat buyers had large orders in mind but never asked, because there was no easy way to.

Use out-of-stock pages

When a product is out of stock, the page usually becomes a dead end. With CatalogX Pro, you can collect enquiries on out-of-stock items instead. A buyer who asks about 50 units of something you are restocking is a warm lead for a quote the moment it comes back.

How to price a quote without guessing

Pricing every quote from scratch is slow and leads to random discounts. A few simple rules make it fast and fair.

  • Start from a floor. Know your lowest acceptable price for each product, after cost and shipping. Never go below it, however nice the buyer is.
  • Use quantity tiers. Decide your discounts by volume in advance, for example 5 percent at 50 units, 10 percent at 100 and 15 percent at 250. Then every quote starts from the same table.
  • Price by customer type. Trade buyers, resellers and one-time buyers deserve different prices. CatalogX Pro lets you set role-based and customer-specific prices, so repeat buyers see their price without asking.
  • Add a reason to act now. An expiry date of 14 or 30 days protects you from cost changes and gives the buyer a reason to decide.
  • Listen to the offered price. CatalogX Pro lets buyers suggest the price they want. You do not have to accept it, but it tells you what the buyer has in mind, and you can counter from there.

Tip: once your quote prices settle, move the most common ones into rules. If you always give trade buyers 15 percent off, set that as a wholesale or role-based price, and save quotes for the deals that really need a conversation.

What happens after a buyer accepts

The moment a buyer accepts a quote is the moment the deal is won, but it is also where many manual processes break. If someone has to retype the quote into an order, mistakes creep in, and the buyer waits.

With CatalogX Pro, an accepted quote becomes a normal WooCommerce order at the agreed price. From there, everything you already use keeps working: stock levels update, the order shows in your reports, shipping plugins pick it up and invoices are created as usual. The buyer can see the order and the original quote side by side in their account.

Two small habits make this step smoother:

  • Confirm the next steps. Send a short message with the delivery date and payment terms, even if the order email covers it. It reassures the buyer that a real person is handling their order.
  • Save the deal for next time. If the buyer is likely to order again, set their agreed price as a customer-specific price. Next time they can order straight away, without another quote.

Using quotes alongside wholesale pricing

Quotes and wholesale pricing are not rivals. They work best together. Wholesale pricing handles the repeat orders where the price is already agreed. Quotes handle the new, large or unusual orders where it is not.

A common setup looks like this:

  • Retail visitors see normal prices and Add to Cart.
  • Approved wholesale buyers log in and see their wholesale price, with minimum order quantities and a fast order form.
  • Everyone can request a quote for very large orders or custom work.

With CatalogX Pro, the wholesale customer role, minimum quantities, the wholesale order form and quotes all share the same rules. You can also hide retail prices from wholesale buyers, so they only see the price that applies to them.

This mix lets small orders flow through checkout with no work from your team, while the big orders get the personal attention they deserve.

Five example setups, by type of store

Settings are easier to choose when you can picture a store like yours. Here are five common setups. They are examples, not rules, so mix and match what fits.

A furniture showroom

The store sells sofas, tables and lighting to homeowners, and the same products to interior designers and hotels. Homeowners pay the listed price. Designers expect a trade discount, and hotels order dozens of the same item.

Setup: keep Add to Cart for everyone and add a Request a Quote button beside it. Create a trade role for designers so they see their own price without asking. Use the quote list for hotel projects, where the buyer adds twenty products in different quantities and sends one request. Add a delivery date dropdown to the form, because big projects run to a deadline.

Why it works: retail sales stay fully automatic, while the large orders, where a little attention makes a big difference, go to the sales team.

A print and signage shop

Every job is a little different: size, material, number of colors, finishing and quantity. Listing every combination as a variation would create thousands of options and still miss some.

Setup: hide Add to Cart on custom products and show only the quote button. Keep a few fixed-price best sellers, such as standard business cards, on normal checkout. Add a file upload for artwork and a dropdown for deadline. Send quotes as PDFs with a 14-day expiry, because paper and ink prices move.

Why it works: the buyer describes the job once, with the artwork attached, and the shop can price it in minutes instead of exchanging five emails.

An industrial parts supplier

Buyers are engineers and purchasing managers. They know part numbers, need formal documents for approval and rarely pay by card. Prices depend on the account and the volume.

Setup: run the store in catalog mode, with prices visible only to logged-in, approved accounts. Everyone can request a quote. Approved accounts get customer-specific prices and see their quotes in My Account. Quote acceptance turns into an order that the finance team invoices as usual.

Why it works: the website becomes a self-service catalog that feeds the sales team with complete, ready-to-price requests, and buyers get the formal quote their purchasing process needs.

A wholesale food and drink brand

The brand sells to cafés, shops and restaurants. Most orders repeat each week at agreed prices, but new stockists and seasonal bulk orders need a conversation.

Setup: use the wholesale customer role with minimum order quantities and the wholesale order form for repeat orders. Hide retail prices from wholesale buyers. Add a quote button for new stockists and for orders above a set size, so the team can offer launch deals and delivery slots.

Why it works: weekly orders take a few clicks with no work from the team, and the sales effort goes into growing new accounts.

An event rental company

Customers rent chairs, tables, tents and lighting for a date. The price depends on the items, the number of days, the distance and the setup work.

Setup: replace Add to Cart with Request a Quote on every product. Let customers build a quote list of everything they need for the event. Add fields for event date, venue postcode and whether they need setup. Reply with two options, with and without setup, and an expiry date a week before the event.

Why it works: customers plan the whole event in one list, and the company prices the full job, including delivery and labor, in a single quote.

Quote reply templates you can copy

Writing every reply from scratch is slow. Save a few short templates and adjust them for each buyer. Here are three that work well.

The first quote

Hi Sam,

Thanks for your request for 40 Oak Dining Tables. Your quote is attached and also waiting in your account.

The price includes 10 percent off for orders of 25 or more, and free delivery to your address. It is valid until December 10.

If you would like to change quantities, finishes or delivery dates, just reply here and I will update it the same day.

Best,
Priya

The friendly follow-up

Hi Sam,

Just checking you got the quote for the 40 Oak Dining Tables. It is valid until December 10.

If the timing or budget has changed, let me know. I am happy to split the order or suggest a similar table at a lower price.

Best,
Priya

The reply to a rejection

Hi Sam,

Thanks for letting us know. Could you tell me in one line what did not work? Price, timing or something else?

It helps us improve, and if it was price, I may be able to suggest another option.

Best,
Priya

Keep templates short. Buyers skim, and a long email full of sales talk feels like pressure. The quote itself carries the details.

Quotes in a multivendor marketplace

If you run a marketplace on WooCommerce, quotes get one more layer: each request has to reach the right vendor. CatalogX works with MultiVendorX, so buyers can request quotes on vendor products and each vendor can handle the requests for their own items.

For marketplaces, keep the rules simple and the same for every vendor at the start. Ask vendors to agree to a reply time, and remind buyers on the form when they will hear back. A marketplace where some vendors reply in an hour and others in a week feels unreliable, even if most vendors are great.

Launch checklist

Before you announce the new quote button, run through this list.

  • The button shows on the right products, and only for the right people.
  • The button text says what the buyer gets, such as "Request a quote".
  • The form asks only for what you need to price the order.
  • The form says when the buyer will hear back.
  • Spam protection is on.
  • You sent a test request and received the email and the request in the admin.
  • You tested the full flow on a phone.
  • Your team knows who replies to quotes, and how fast.
  • You saved your reply templates.
  • You know your floor prices and volume tiers.

10 common mistakes to avoid

  1. Hiding prices everywhere. If most of your buyers would pay the listed price, hiding it costs you sales. Hide it only where it would be wrong or off-putting.
  2. Using vague button text. "Inquire" or "Submit" does not tell the buyer what they get. "Request a quote" or "Get bulk pricing" does.
  3. Asking for too much up front. A 15-field form scares buyers off. Ask for the minimum, then follow up.
  4. Not saying when you will reply. Silence after a request feels like a black hole. Set and show a reply time.
  5. Replying with a plain email. A loose email with a number in it is hard to approve. A clear quote document with lines and totals is easy.
  6. No expiry date. Without one, a buyer can come back six months later expecting an old price.
  7. Retyping quotes into orders. Every manual copy is a chance for a wrong price or quantity. Let accepted quotes become orders automatically.
  8. Forgetting mobile. Many buyers browse on a phone. Test your quote button and form on a small screen.
  9. No follow-up. Many lost quotes are just forgotten ones. One kind reminder recovers a good share of them.
  10. Not tracking results. If you do not know how many quotes turn into orders, you cannot improve. See the next section.

What to measure

You do not need a complex dashboard. Track these five numbers each month and you will know if your quote process is working.

Quote requests

How many requests came in. Rising is good, as long as quality holds.

Reply time

Average time from request to first quote. Lower is better.

Win rate

Accepted quotes divided by quotes sent. Your most important number.

Average quote value

Tells you if quotes bring bigger orders than checkout does.

Top reasons for rejection

Price, timing or fit. Tells you what to fix next.

Compare these with your normal checkout orders. If quoted orders are larger on average, and your win rate is healthy, the quote button is doing its job. If the win rate is low, look at reply time and quote clarity before you cut prices.

Can buyers ask for a quote on many products at once?

Yes. Buyers add products to a quote list, set quantities and variations, and send everything as one request.

With CatalogX Pro, yes. When the buyer accepts a quote, it becomes a normal WooCommerce order at the agreed price.

Can I send quotes as PDFs?

Yes, with CatalogX Pro. Buyers can view and download a branded PDF of their quote.

Yes. CatalogX is compatible with WPML, so buttons, forms and emails can be translated.

Both. You can let every visitor request quotes, or limit quotes to logged-in customers or certain roles later on.

Can a buyer suggest their own price?

Most stores use 14 to 30 days. Use a shorter window if your costs change often, and a longer one for large projects with long approval times.
